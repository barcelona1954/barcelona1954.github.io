<details class="pv-reveal">
  <summary>My Story</summary>
<p>
  I remember watching The Beatles premier on The Ed Sullivan Show in 1964. I was at my Grandparents house, and it was a cold and gloomy February evening in southern Indiana. But that music. And those screaming girls. And that "long hair" on the lads from Liverpool that seemed to shock the adults in the room. I guess these things left an impression. 
  </p>
  <p>
  Although my Dad was a an excellent singer&mdash;a skill he'd developed from his aunt, a music teacher, my family wasn't otherwise particularly musical. There weren't any instruments or music in our house growing up. But by they time I was in 4th grade or so, the "British Invasion" was in full swing and I decided I wanted to be part of the action. If only I could play <i>Day Tripper</i> or <i>Ticket To Ride</i>, I was sure my life and elementary school prestige would launch. So, I convinced my parents to let me take guitar lessons. 
</p>
<p>
  We somehow scored a cheap acoustic guitar and I started learning guitar from an a crew-cut cowboy, more Buck Owens than John Lennon. He taught me to read music and play basic chords, and I developed muscle memory for moving my fingers around the fret board. Lamentably, I was mired in a Mel Bay guitar book learning <i>Greensleeves</i> and the <i>Marine's Hymn</i> rather than the songs I heard on AM radio. Those were the ones that I wanted to play. So, after a year or so, I quit. 
  </p>
<p>
In high school, I fell in with several long-haired friends who played guitar. I bought another acoustic guitar and started learning songs by Bob Dylan, Donovan, and Leonard Cohen. The muscle memory from my youth served me well, but I  knew precious little about about music theory, scales, or even what it meant to play in key. After a couple years of fiddling around, I drifted away from guitar again, this time to pursue art and travel.  
</p>
<p>
Many years later, my kids, all accomplished musicians themselves, encouraged me to give the guitar another try. I coaxed a friend, co-worker, and accomplished musician, Craig, into offering after-work guitar tutorials. Then signed up for lessons at Graner Music, where I'd spent years dropping off my kids for theirs. My family bought me an Ovation acoustic guitar for Christmas, and I finally began establishing a regular practice routine.
</p>
<p>
One day at work, probably in the early 2010s, I was chatting with my friend Brian by the break-room coffee machine and mentioned that I was taking guitar lessons. What a coincidence! He was taking bass guitar lessons. We decided to try playing some songs together. I'd never played music with anybody else before, but it sounded like fun.
</p>
<p>
So one winter evening, Brian came over to my house, and we gave it a go.

Now, because I thought bands were supposed to practice in the garage, I'd pulled one car out to make room for our amps and a couple of chairs. And there we played a pathetically poor man's version of Santana's Europa (I don't recommend starting with that one!) and one of Creedence Clearwater Revival's "rain" songs. It wasn't until our fingers were chilled and our limbs shivering that I had an epiphany: Hey, I own this house. We can play in any room I want!
</p>
<p>
So, for the next several years, we got together every few weeks, trading off between our houses—indoors! We never performed or tried playing outside our living rooms, but for fun, we called ourselves Al Palomino and the Pintos. Over time, we built up a playlist of maybe 15 songs that we could muddle through over cocktails and Friday night happy hours.
</p>
<p>
Then one day, Brian proposed an experiment. He invited a mutual friend, Chuck, who'd been playing in bands since he was knee-high to a harpsichord, to join us. The challenge: While our wives went out for dinner and drinks, we'd learn a song and play it for them when they returned.
</p>
<p>
I was skeptical, but game.
</p>
<p>
We picked <i>Surf Nouveau</i>, originally recorded by a group called the Aqua Velvets. Thanks to Chuck's amazing musical ear and Brian's sense of rhythm, we worked out the chords and melody, found a suitable beat on the drum machine, and rehearsed it a dozen times or so. Slowly at first, then at something approaching the original tempo. When the ladies returned from their night out, we played it for them.
</p>
<p>
 Not bad. 
 </p>
 <p>
 So we played it again. 
 </p>
 <p>
 Kinda fun. 
 </p>
 <p>
 And, by popular request, we played it again. 
 </p>
 <p>
 The ladies were dancing. 
 </p>
 <p>
 Not exactly Ed Sullivan, but I was feeling some of those vibes.
 </p>
 <p>
 And that's how the Corpse Revivers (Brian, Chuck, a drum machine, and me) were born. 
</p>

</details>
